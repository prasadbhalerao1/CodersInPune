Round 1 submission · The Plan

Relay

Handovers you can check, correct and rehearse before the person leaves

When someone leaves a software project, the code stays but the reasons, the unfinished work and the people to call usually go with them. Relay connects to a team's GitHub organisation, rebuilds the state of one responsibility from evidence, shows exactly what it could not find, lets a person correct and approve it, and gives the incoming person a version built only from what they are allowed to see. Then the incoming person tries their first real task inside Relay, so the gaps show up while the outgoing person can still answer.

1Understanding the problem

Every team we have worked in has had the same moment. Someone who "just knew how things worked" moves on, and the person who takes over spends the first weeks asking questions in chat, reading old pull requests and guessing which README is still true. The repository survived. The understanding did not.

1.1 Who loses knowledge

Three groups lose something, and they lose different things:

  • The incoming person loses time and confidence. They inherit a responsibility without the reasons behind it and cannot tell which documents are current.
  • The team lead loses visibility. They cannot tell whether a handover is actually complete or just looks complete because a document exists.
  • The organisation loses continuity. Recurring incidents get rediscovered, decisions get reversed by accident, and work that was 80% done gets abandoned because nobody knew it existed.

The outgoing person loses something too: their last two weeks get eaten by writing a handover document from memory, usually in a hurry, with no idea what the next person will actually need.

1.2 What exactly is lost

1.3 Evidence that this hurts real teams

  • A 2018 survey of more than 1,000 US knowledge workers (Panopto with YouGov) found that employees spend about 5.3 hours a week waiting for information from colleagues or recreating knowledge that already exists, and that roughly 42% of institutional knowledge is unique to the individual holding it [R1]. The study was commissioned by a vendor, so we treat the numbers as a signal, not a precise measurement.
  • Avelino, Valente and Hora estimated the "truck factor" of 133 popular GitHub projects: the number of developers who would have to leave before the project stalls. 46% had a truck factor of 1 and 28% had a truck factor of 2 [R2]. Even well-known open-source projects depend heavily on a few people.
  • Student clubs and college project teams have a built-in version of this problem: every year the people who built the system graduate. The new batch inherits a repository and a WhatsApp group.
What we have not done yet. We have not run formal interviews. Before Round 2 we will interview at least five people who recently inherited a project and five who handed one over, and ask each to walk us through the first task they could not do on their own. We will report what we find, including anything that contradicts this plan (Section 10.2).

1.6 The part of the problem we will solve

The brief is wide. We are deliberately choosing one slice and doing it properly:

Our scope: a software team that uses GitHub transfers one named responsibility (for example "maintain the payment callback service") from one person to another. Relay reconstructs that responsibility from the team's GitHub organisation, shows the evidence and the gaps, gets a human to approve it, gives the recipient a permission-correct version, and lets them rehearse the first task.

We are not trying to capture all of an organisation's knowledge, read people's private messages, judge employee performance, or detect who is about to resign. Section 12 lists the limits in full.

2Users affected

Our first users are small software teams, research engineering groups and student product teams that already coordinate in GitHub: two to ten repositories, identifiable maintainers, and at least one real change of ownership coming up.

Kabir, incoming maintainer

Joined the payments team last week. Has read access to the service repo. Needs to answer: what am I responsible for, what can break, who can help, and what do I do first?

Riya, outgoing maintainer

Moving to another team in ten days. Knows the service well but has no time to write a 20-page doc. Wants to answer a few precise questions, not start from a blank page.

Anand, team lead

Has to sign off that the transfer is safe. Needs to see what is still unknown, who owns each next step, and exactly which version the newcomer acknowledged.

These are illustrative personas for a fictional team, not interview subjects.

2.2 Situations we design for

  • Planned move: someone changes team or finishes an internship, and there are one to two weeks of overlap.
  • Unexpected absence: the outgoing person is unavailable. Relay builds what the evidence supports, routes questions to another maintainer, and never invents an answer or signs on the absent person's behalf.
  • Batch turnover: a student club or research group hands a system to the next cohort at the end of the year.

3Proposed solution

3.1 The idea in one paragraph

A useful handover needs three things: an accurate account of the current state, an honest list of what is still unknown, and a way for the recipient to try the job before they have to rely on it. Relay produces all three for one responsibility at a time. A responsibility ties together code paths, documents, decisions, open work, risks and people. Relay fills it from GitHub evidence, checks the documents against the repository, asks the outgoing person only about real gaps, and publishes a version that a reviewer approved and that the recipient is allowed to read.

3.2 What the outcome looks like

The output is a short, approved handover with five sections (current state, key decisions and reasons, watch-outs, people and dependencies, next three moves), a separate gap list, and a rehearsal checklist. Every statement carries its evidence and its status. A sample is in Section 4.2.

3.3 What makes Relay different from a summariser

A generic tool reads documents and writes a summary. That summary is fluent, sounds certain, and cannot be checked. Relay is built around five ideas we have not seen combined elsewhere:

IdeaWhy it matters
Responsibility, not repository or person, is the unit"Hand over everything Riya knows" is impossible to finish. "Hand over the payment callback service" has a clear boundary and a clear definition of done.
Handover rehearsalThe recipient walks through a real first task (for example, diagnosing duplicate payment callbacks) using only the handover. Wherever they get stuck becomes an owned follow-up, found while the outgoing person can still answer.
Documentation is checked against the repositoryDeterministic rules find runbooks that point at deleted files, docs whose code changed after their last review, and changes that needed a decision record but have none. These are facts, not AI guesses.
Four trust labels instead of one "verified" badgeEach claim separately shows evidence support, human review, freshness, and access lineage. "Approved last week but the source changed yesterday" is visible instead of hidden.
The recipient's version is built, not redactedIf the lead can see repo B and the recipient cannot, the recipient's handover is generated only from what they can read. Removing citations after generation would still leak B's content through the wording.

3.4 Value for each user

  • Outgoing person: answers about five precise questions instead of writing from scratch.
  • Incoming person: gets a short plan where every claim opens to its source, and practises the first task before it matters.
  • Lead: sees concrete counts ("6 of 8 procedures reviewed, 2 blockers open") and an exact record of what was approved and acknowledged.
  • Organisation: documentation drift is caught during normal work, so the next departure starts from a better place.

4Complete product workflow

The workflow follows the five steps the brief requires and adds a sixth, Rehearse, which is where most of our originality sits.

4.1 Walkthrough with a concrete example

Our demo uses a fictional company, Tidewater Pay, in a GitHub organisation we create and seed ourselves. Riya is handing the payment callback service to Kabir.

  1. Connect. The admin installs the Relay GitHub App on two of the org's five repositories. The lead creates a transition: responsibility "payment-operations", outgoing Riya, incoming Kabir, reviewer Anand, target date 20 November. Relay confirms that Kabir can read payment-service but not finance-ops.
  2. Reconstruct. Relay reads the runbook, ADRs, 90 days of commits, open issues and PRs touching src/payments/**. It drafts claims such as "callbacks are deduplicated with idempotency keys since ADR-007", each linked to the exact file and commit.
  3. Verify. Rule R03 finds that the runbook points to scripts/payment-rollback.sh, which does not exist at the current commit. The model spots a candidate conflict: the runbook says 5 retries, PR #212 changed it to 3. Relay shows both excerpts and picks neither.
  4. Review. Riya gets four questions, including "Which retry count applies to the current release, and what replaced the rollback script?" Riya answers two, marks one as unknown and routes one to another maintainer. Anand corrects one claim and approves version 3.
  5. Handover. Relay builds Kabir's version only from payment-service. A cost note that came from finance-ops is absent: not blurred, not counted, simply absent. Kabir opens it, inspects evidence and acknowledges version 3.
  6. Rehearse. Kabir works through "diagnose a duplicate callback" step by step. At checkpoint 3 Kabir cannot open the provider dashboard. That becomes a blocker owned by Anand. The transfer is not marked complete until it is resolved or Anand explicitly accepts the risk.

6.2 Architecture

One API service and one background worker, built as clear modules. Microservices would add failure modes without helping a three-person team.

6.5 Evidence model

Every statement in a handover is a claim. A claim is never just text; it points to the exact piece of source it came from, and it carries four independent labels.

A URL is not enough provenance: files change. We pin evidence to a commit SHA and a line range, and issue comments to a stored snapshot hash, because comments can be edited after the fact.

6.6 How AI is used, and where it is not

The model is allowed toDeterministic code always owns
Extract candidate decisions, risks and procedures from permitted chunksWho can see what (policy engine)
Draft the narrative around already-accepted claimsWhich revision is being analysed
Suggest possible contradictions between two statementsWhether a cited span actually exists in the source
Propose gap questionsRule results R01 to R07
Approval, publishing and completion state

Model output must validate against a JSON schema and every citation must resolve to a stored evidence span, or the output is rejected. When evidence is missing, the right answer is "we don't know, and here is the gap", and the system is built to produce exactly that. If the model is down, the rules, the manual editor and every approved handover keep working.

7Security, privacy and permissions

The brief puts it plainly: do not expose confidential information just because it appeared in a connected source. This section is how we keep that promise.

7.1 The access decision

A read is allowed only when all of these are true: the user is signed in and active in the workspace; the project policy lets them see this item; GitHub says they can read the source repository (checked with their own linked GitHub identity, not the installation token); the repository is still in the installation's scope; and, for anything derived from several sources, they pass the requirement of every source that shaped it. GitHub access is the ceiling. Relay can restrict further, never widen.

If the access check itself fails (GitHub is down, a token expired), we deny the source-derived content and say why. Failing open would be the easy bug to write and the worst one to ship.

7.2 Recipient versions instead of redaction

A claim that used both A and B is not shown to the recipient with B's citation removed. Either it is regenerated from A alone with new lineage, or it is left out. The recipient's view does not reveal names, titles or counts of anything they cannot see, not even "3 items hidden".

7.4 Data handling and people's trust

  • Demo data is entirely synthetic, in an organisation we own. We will never ask judges or users to upload confidential work material.
  • Tokens live server-side only; the GitHub App private key sits in the host's secret store. Logs carry IDs, never source text, prompts or tokens.
  • Proposed pilot retention: source snapshots 90 days, approved handovers one year, raw webhook payloads seven days. Deleting a source denies access immediately and removes derived chunks, vectors and drafts.
  • Exports are a known limit: once someone downloads a PDF, revocation cannot pull it back. Relay says so before every export.
  • Relay tracks responsibilities, not people. No productivity scores, no rankings, no "this person seems to be leaving" alerts. Commit volume can suggest a candidate owner; only a lead can confirm one. People can correct how their identities are mapped.

9.3 Three improvements we commit to for Round 3

Reliable webhooks, a 15-minute reconciliation sweep for missed events, and drift-triggered review: when watched code changes, the affected handover is flagged "changed since review" and a new revision is proposed.

Tests: T06, T07, T10, T14

2. Permission hardening

Revocation that invalidates derived views, caches and exports; policy epochs on reinstall; export safety checks on the actual bytes of the PDF and Markdown.

Tests: T04, T05, T16, T17

3. Richer rehearsal

Reviewer-defined checkpoints, blocker ownership and due dates, version-specific acknowledgement, and completion rules the lead configures (with named, expiring risk acceptance).

10Expected impact and how we will measure it

We will not promise a percentage of "knowledge captured". That number cannot be measured honestly. We will measure things we can count, against the team's own baseline.

MeasureHow we count itTarget for a pilot
Handover preparation timeMinutes from choosing sources to an approved handover, excluding idle timeAt least 30% lower than the team's current process
Supported claimsMaterial claims with valid evidence ÷ all material claims, on a human-labelled set95% or higher
Rule precisionCorrect actionable findings ÷ all rule findings reviewed90% or higher
Access safetyRestricted content exposed in our adversarial test suiteZero; any exposure blocks release
Reviewer effortActive minutes to review and correct one handoverMedian 15 minutes or less
First-task successRecipients who finish the rehearsal without outside helpBetter than a comparable baseline; sample size reported

These are targets, not results. With small samples we will report what we saw, including what Relay missed.

10.1 Why it matters

  • For the newcomer: the first week starts with a short, checkable plan and a practised first task instead of a scavenger hunt.
  • For the team: the outgoing person's last days go into answering five real questions, not writing from memory.
  • For the organisation: drift checks run during normal work, so documentation improves before anyone leaves. That is what turns a one-off handover tool into something used every week.
  • For student clubs and research groups: yearly turnover stops resetting the project to zero.

10.2 How we will validate the problem before Round 2

Vipul leads ten short interviews: five people who recently inherited a project and five who handed one over (students, interns and working developers we can reach). Each is asked to reconstruct a real transfer, name the first task they could not do alone, show which sources they checked, and estimate the time spent searching. We use only material they are allowed to share. Findings, including any that push against this plan, go into the Round 2 document.

11Future scope

After the event we would run a six-week pilot with one or two real teams, then grow only where the pilot shows demand. Rough order:

  1. Pilot hardening: multiple projects per workspace, GitHub check runs on PRs (opt-in, same-repo facts only), review queues and notifications.
  2. More sources, each with its own permission model: Jira or Linear for tasks, Notion and Confluence pages, Slack threads that a user explicitly links. No bulk import of anyone's messages.
  3. Documentation pull requests: Relay proposes a fix for a drifted runbook as a normal PR that humans review. Separate write permission, separate consent.
  4. Sandboxed rehearsal: let the recipient actually run a task in an isolated environment, with its own threat model.
  5. Enterprise: SSO, GitHub Enterprise Server, regional hosting, configurable retention.

A plausible model is a workspace subscription with a repository allowance. Charging per departure would discourage the routine upkeep that makes the product valuable, so we would avoid it.

What Relay cannot do, and will say so in the product

  • It cannot recover knowledge that was never written down. It can only find the gap and ask.
  • Approval is an accountable human decision, not proof that something is true.
  • Commits do not prove understanding, and a document's existence does not prove a service is safe to run.
  • A downloaded export cannot be recalled after access is revoked.
  • Attribution from commit history is weak (squash merges, bots, pair programming), so ownership is always a suggestion until a lead confirms it.
